# Prose Craft

Write from the source packet. Do not use fluency to fill a hole in the thinking.

## Drafting rules

1. Start with the clearest true claim available.
2. Prefer the user's approved words when they already say the thing well.
3. Use concrete nouns and active, specific verbs.
4. Make every abstraction earn its place with an example, mechanism, consequence, definition, or named case.
5. Keep one main idea per sentence. Let sentence length follow the thought.
6. Use transitions only when they express a real relationship: cause, contrast, sequence, concession, or consequence.
7. Preserve uncertainty, limits, and qualifications when they matter.
8. Do not create anecdotes, proof, motives, feelings, or personal details.

## The commodity test

Ask of every important sentence: could this appear unchanged in ten thousand posts, reports, or essays on the topic?

If yes, either anchor it to a specific fact, distinction, example, mechanism, or consequence, or delete it. Necessary connective sentences may remain plain. Do not decorate them to make them seem original.

## The plain-language pass

Prefer the ordinary word when it carries the same meaning. Replace vague abstractions with the thing that actually happened. Name the actor. Name the action. Name the object.

Run four Graham checks:
- **Friction:** can the reader reach the idea without spending attention decoding the sentence?
- **Honesty:** if the sentence is rewritten plainly, is there still a worthwhile idea underneath?
- **Natural rhythm:** does the sentence length and shape follow the thought, or a writing pattern?
- **Meaning:** when a sentence sounds wrong, ask what you actually mean before changing vocabulary.

For idea-development writing, reread the important passages repeatedly. Try making an awkward paragraph slightly shorter. If the rewrite changes the idea, prefer the version that is more precise and true. Do not preserve a sentence merely because it sounds polished.

Cut:
- throat-clearing and generic scene-setting;
- ornamental metaphors that add no understanding;
- abstract noun chains;
- inflated verbs and prestige vocabulary;
- generic headings;
- repeated conclusions;
- fake candor, fake profundity, and forced punchlines;
- rhetorical symmetry that exists for sound rather than meaning;
- motivational filler and corporate language unsupported by the source.

Do not hunt for synonyms merely to sound varied. Repetition is acceptable when the repeated word is the precise word.

## Form-match pass

After clarity, ask whether the form should remain plain or become marked.

A marked device includes long sentences, fragments, repetition, rhetorical questions, parallelism, chiasmus, metaphor, unusual punctuation, deliberate ambiguity, non-linear structure, or strong cadence.

Default to the simpler form. Keep a marked device only when you can name the work it does: clarify a relationship, embody an experience, create escalation, improve memory, reveal contrast, make scale visible, lower defensiveness, or carry necessary tone.

Do not normalize a passage merely because a style rule dislikes it. If removing the device makes the meaning or experience weaker, preserve it.

## Voice

Voice comes from approved samples, not from mixing famous authors. A writing authority may change a decision about structure, research, compression, or clarity. It does not automatically change diction.

Read aloud. If a sentence sounds like something written for the idea of an audience rather than something a thoughtful person would actually say, rebuild it from the underlying claim.

Preserve material claims and qualifications. Respect necessary academic and technical terms. Edit destructively when needed, but never polish away an approved writer's useful idiosyncrasy.
## Contrast examples

These examples teach decisions. Do not reuse their wording automatically.

**Empty abstraction**
Weak: "This approach creates a more robust and aligned operating model."
Better: "Product and sales now use the same definition of a qualified lead."
Reason: the second sentence names the change. The first only praises it.

**Prestige vocabulary**
Weak: "We utilised a multifaceted framework to facilitate stakeholder alignment."
Better: "We used one scoring model so the three teams judged candidates the same way."
Reason: ordinary words expose the actual mechanism.

**Commodity insight**
Weak: "Consistency is the key to long-term success."
Better: delete it unless the piece can explain what must stay consistent, why it matters, and what happened when it did not.
Reason: a fluent commonplace is still a commonplace.

**Claim without mechanism**
Weak: "AI will transform recruiting."
Better: "If screening becomes cheap, recruiters spend less time sorting applicants and more time deciding what evidence actually predicts performance."
Reason: name the causal chain.

**Complex sentence with no need**
Weak: "Given the increasingly complex nature of the environment, organisations must seek to develop capabilities that enable more effective adaptation."
Better: "The market changes faster now. Teams need to notice those changes earlier and respond faster."
Reason: separate ideas unless their relationship requires one sentence.

**Rhythm exposing thought**
Weak: "The product failed because the team moved too slowly, lacked alignment, had weak positioning, and because customers were confused."
Better: first decide whether these are four causes, one cause with symptoms, or a sequence. Then write that structure.
Reason: awkward rhythm can be evidence that the thinking is still unresolved.


## Business-writing pass

When the piece is practical business prose, run the Adams pass after the idea is clear:

- Delete intensifiers and filler before changing anything else.
- Split sentences that carry two independent thoughts.
- Prefer actor, action, object when that order makes the sentence easier to picture.
- Rewrite the opening several times. Keep the version that creates the clearest reason to read the next sentence.
- If five sentences can carry the argument and evidence, do not use fifteen.
- Use the more exact word when it changes the image or tone. Do not use a rarer word merely to sound distinctive.

Short is not automatically good. A sentence may be long when the relationship between its parts is the point. Brevity serves comprehension and persuasion. It is not a word-count contest.

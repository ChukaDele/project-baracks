# Brand identity design

Do not start with the logo. The logo is step four.

## Prerequisite

You need an approved positioning and messaging. If they are missing, use `brand-strategy` first. Design without them produces taste arguments.

Also collect the competitive visual audit: 8–15 real competitor screenshots, with their colours, type, imagery style, and layout logic. This audit is the only valid test of "different".

## Step 1: Visual territories (Stylescapes)

Chris Do and Jose Caballer's method, taught at The Futur.

1. Define the desired future state: what a buyer should think and feel in three seconds.
2. Convert positioning into 5–7 visual attributes. Each attribute must be visual, not abstract. "Precise" is usable. "Innovative" is not.
3. Build image buckets: photography, texture, colour, type, layout, motion, environment.
4. Compose two or three wide boards. Each board is one complete world, not a mood collage.
5. Make the boards genuinely different from each other. Two boards that differ only in colour are one board.
6. Present as a compass direction, not a final design. Ask which world the buyer should live in.

Check each board against the competitor audit. If a board could belong to a competitor, kill it.

## Step 2: Typography (Lupton, Spiekermann, Scher)

1. Choose type for purpose first: reading, interface, display, and documents.
2. Build the scale: sizes, weights, line height, line length, and spacing rules.
3. Pair with Lupton's rule: enough contrast to be clear, enough shared structure to look intentional. Two families is usually enough.
4. Set hierarchy rules for headline, subhead, body, caption, label, and data.
5. Check production reality (Spiekermann): licence cost, web loading, small sizes, signage, and screen rendering.
6. Consider type as the identity itself (Scher) when the budget cannot support a strong symbol.

Test at 11px on a phone, in a black and white print, and in a slide seen from four metres.

## Step 3: Colour (Albers, WCAG, Romaniuk)

1. Never pick colour from a colour-meaning chart. State this to the client once.
2. Build the palette in context. Albers's rule: a colour changes with its neighbour, its area, and its light. Test each colour beside the others, in large and small areas.
3. Define roles: primary, secondary, accent, background, text, states, and data colours.
4. Check accessibility against WCAG 2.2 for text and non-text contrast. Record the ratios. Fix failures before approval.
5. Check ownability with Romaniuk's question: could this colour ever become ours in this category? If three competitors already own it, choose again or make it work through combination and consistency.
6. Test in bad conditions: dark mode, low-quality print, screenshots, and compressed video.

## Step 4: Logo and wordmark (Haviv)

1. Sketch wide and cheap first. Volume before polish.
2. Judge with three criteria: appropriate to the organisation, distinctive and memorable, simple in form.
3. Do not explain the whole business in the mark. A logo identifies. It does not narrate.
4. Avoid the literal-metaphor trap. For a foundry, avoid molten metal. For AI, avoid neural nodes. For recruitment, avoid people-shaped chains.
5. Build the lock-up set: primary, horizontal, stacked, icon, favicon, and a one-colour version.
6. Test at 16px, in embroidery, in a stamp, and in a black square.

## Step 5: Distinctive asset palette (Romaniuk)

Build 3–5 recognition assets beyond the logo. Choose from: colour combination, type treatment, shape or graphic device, layout signature, character or mascot, image style, sound, tagline phrase, and packaging or format.

Score each asset on two axes:

- **Fame:** how many buyers link it to us.
- **Uniqueness:** how many name only us.

For a new brand, Fame is zero by definition. So select for high potential Uniqueness, then commit to repetition. Write a consistency rule: what will not change for three years.

## Step 6: Identity system (Wheeler, The Futur)

Deliver the applications the business actually uses. For a small B2B service firm, that usually means: website, proposal and pitch deck, LinkedIn and profile assets, email signature, invoice and contract, one-pagers, case study template, and social templates.

Do not build packaging, vehicle livery, or signage for a business that has none.

## Step 7: Brand guide

Include: strategy summary, logo rules, type system, colour system with contrast data, asset palette with consistency rules, imagery rules, layout grid, application examples, do-and-do-not pages, and file locations.

Keep it usable. A 90-page guide that nobody opens is a failure.

## Validation gates

1. Which strategic input caused this decision?
2. Which alternatives did we kill?
3. Is it different from the actual competitor audit?
4. Does it pass WCAG contrast?
5. Does it work at 16px, in one colour, and in bad print?
6. Does it look like a default AI or template aesthetic? If yes, rebuild.
7. Which two or three assets are we committing to repeat, unchanged?
8. What needs a recognition test with real buyers?

## Limits

- Stylescapes align clients. They do not prove distinctiveness. The audit and the Uniqueness question do that.
- Albers explains behaviour, not brand ownership.
- Craft experts do not overrule positioning, and positioning does not overrule contrast ratios.

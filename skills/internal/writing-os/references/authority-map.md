# Writing authority map

Authorities are decision tools, not costumes. Apply the method that belongs to the problem. Do not blend their surface styles into one synthetic voice.

## Thinking and research

### Robert Greene: investigation, contradiction, and layered case construction

Use Greene when a piece needs depth before it needs prose.

His useful pattern is to investigate the human forces beneath the visible event: incentives, status, fear, ambition, power, timing, imitation, resentment, self-deception, and second-order effects. Gather cases across periods and domains. Look for a case that supports the thesis, a case that complicates it, and a reversal or boundary condition.

The aim is not to collect examples after deciding the answer. The examples should pressure-test the answer and reveal a more precise one.

Greene also offers a useful page architecture: a clear proposition, concrete historical or biographical cases, interpretation, counterexample or reversal, and side material that adds another lens without breaking the main argument.

Owns: research depth, case selection, contradiction, motive, nuance, historical analogy, reversals, and modular page architecture.

Stops at: default diction, severity, moral framing, archaic or theatrical language, and claims about motive that evidence cannot support. Use his thinking process more often than his language.

### Paul Graham: simplicity as a thinking instrument

Use ordinary words and simple sentences unless complexity is doing necessary intellectual work. The goal is low-friction transmission: the reader should spend attention on the idea rather than decoding the prose.

Graham's simplicity test is also an honesty test. Fancy language can disguise a weak idea. Rewrite the claim in plain English. If it becomes obviously empty, fix the thought rather than decorating the sentence.

For idea-development writing, treat sound and thought as coupled. Awkward rhythm can signal that the underlying thought is misshapen. Reread. When a sentence catches, ask what you actually mean. Rewrite until the rhythm follows the shape of the idea rather than a preset cadence.

Use revision as discovery. Making a paragraph slightly shorter, clearer, or better arranged can expose a stronger version of the thought. The writer is the first reader, so repeated rereading is part of reasoning rather than a cosmetic final pass.

Do not outsource the thinking step. For essays and thought leadership, writing is one of the tools by which the writer discovers what they believe. AI may help retrieve, test, arrange, and edit, but should not silently manufacture the central view.

Primary references: "Write Simply" (2021), "Writes and Write-Nots" (2024), and "Good Writing" (2025).

Owns: plain language, intellectual honesty, writing-as-thinking, rereading, natural rhythm, exploratory essays, and revision as a way to improve ideas.

Stops at: sales copy, source verification, and imitation of Graham's personal voice.

### David Perell: write from abundance, test ideas, then compress

Do not start from a blank page when useful material already exists. Gather the writer's notes, conversations, emails, messages, observations, highlights, examples, and prior fragments first. Let patterns in that material suggest the structure.

Use conversation as an idea filter. A useful idea should survive contact with intelligent readers, questions, confusion, and disagreement before it grows into a larger piece.

Find the **Shiny Dime** before drafting: the smallest viable idea the piece is really about. Every section must support, complicate, illustrate, or extend that idea. Tangents are cut even when individually interesting.

Use CRIBS as reader-level editorial feedback:
- Confusing: the writer may not yet understand the point.
- Repetitive: compression is incomplete.
- Interesting: expand or sharpen it.
- Boring: cut it or make the underlying idea more specific.
- Surprising: inspect why it changes the reader's model of the subject.

Personal Monopoly is a topic-selection principle, not a permission slip for generic personal branding. Look for the intersection of actual experience, recurring curiosity, skills, and sustained evidence of interest.

Perell's POP framework can be used as a diagnostic for online writing: Personal, Observational, Playful. Do not force all three into every piece. Personal detail must be true. Observation must add knowledge or a distinct lens. Playfulness is optional and should never become filler.

His later style advice also supplies an important warning for this OS: tools that normalize every sentence can flatten useful variance. Follow grammar and clarity rules deliberately, then preserve intentional idiosyncrasy when it serves the writer.

Primary reference: "The Ultimate Guide to Writing Online."

Owns: idea capture, writing from existing material, conversational testing, the Shiny Dime, reader-level feedback, Personal Monopoly, and preserving useful stylistic variance.

Stops at: source verification, invented personal details, mandatory playfulness, and sentence-level imitation of Perell's own voice.

### Nicholas Cole: approved thinking and the personal language model

Treat generic knowledge as cheap. The valuable corpus is the writer's chosen positions, stories, examples, distinctions, lessons, and language. Let AI retrieve, repeat, recombine, and distribute what has already been thought through. Reserve human attention for net-new thinking and judgment.

Owns: the distinction between commodity content, personality content, approved language, and additive thinking.

Stops at: inventing personality data or treating every repeated phrase as good writing.

### Barbara Minto: answer-first structure

Use the Pyramid Principle when a busy reader needs the answer and grouped support quickly.

Owns: reports, proposals, memos, executive communication, and hierarchy of claims.

Stops at: exploratory essays, narrative, and voice.

## Prose mechanics

### William Zinsser: subtraction

Cut clutter. Prefer the simpler construction when meaning survives.

Owns: the cutting pass.

Stops at: research, argument design, and voice.

### Joseph Williams: sentence flow

Put characters in subjects and actions in verbs. Connect old information to new information so the reader knows what each sentence is doing.

Owns: cohesion and sentence mechanics.

Stops at: the writer's worldview or personality.

### Steven Pinker: reader model and classic style

Help the reader see the thing itself. Watch for the curse of knowledge, unexplained abstractions, and expert shorthand.

Owns: reader comprehension and diagnosing avoidable complexity.

Stops at: personal voice and commercial persuasion.

### Naval Ravikant: compression

Use compression only after the thought has been earned. Short declarative lines can expose whether an idea is clear.

Owns: compression and memorable statements.

Stops at: proof. An aphorism cannot replace reasoning.


### Alex Hormozi: utility, narrow scope, and business-writing simplification

Use Hormozi for practical nonfiction, educational content, frameworks, books that solve a reader problem, and high-stakes launches.

His useful tests are: solve a narrow problem completely; make frameworks both valid and useful; define abstractions through observable behavior when possible; reduce reading friction so cognitive effort goes to the idea; and use stories or examples to carry the experience behind a lesson.

For detailed rules and the four-video source set, read `alex-hormozi.md`.

Owns: practical utility, narrow problem definition, business-writing simplicity, framework stress-testing, behavioral definitions, ideal-reader feedback, destructive editing, and launch preparation.

Stops at: universal sentence-length rules, literary voice, technical terminology that must remain precise, and treating every piece as a problem-solving manual.

### Scott Adams: business-writing mechanics

Use Adams when the job is to make practical prose clearer and more persuasive through subtraction.

His useful rules are mechanical:
- remove words that do no work;
- prefer a short argument to a long argument when both carry the same proof;
- keep one main thought per sentence;
- rewrite the first sentence until it gives the reader a reason to continue;
- prefer direct actor-action-object order when it is clearer than passive or inverted syntax;
- choose a more exact word when tone matters, especially in humor.

The opening rule does not mean every first sentence needs clickbait. It must create useful forward motion: curiosity, tension, a concrete claim, a surprising fact, or immediate relevance.

Treat the claim that every brain processes syntax identically as rhetoric rather than a scientific law. Keep the practical heuristic. Do not import the universal claim.

Primary reference: "The Day You Became A Better Writer" (2007).

Owns: pruning, short business sentences, one-thought-per-sentence discipline, direct syntax, and opening-line economy.

Stops at: research depth, source verification, long-form argument discovery, and universal claims about cognition.

## Operating rules

1. Thought process and language are separate variables.
2. Research authorities may change what questions are asked and how evidence is arranged.
3. Prose authorities may change sentence mechanics without changing the underlying claim.
4. Voice comes from approved samples from the intended writer or brand.
5. Famous writers are never merged into a composite persona.
6. A named authority cannot supply missing evidence, experience, or biography.
7. Specific examples outrank stylistic resemblance.
8. The final piece should sound like one person thinking clearly, not like a committee of admired authors.

# Process evidence from working writers

Use this reference to decide how the writing process should run. These are working methods and hypotheses, not universal laws. Apply them only when they solve the task in front of you.

## 1. Think before polishing

Sam Altman treats writing as externalized thinking. Conversation can generate material, but forcing an idea into a short, internally consistent document exposes weak points that can remain hidden in speech.

Operational rule:
- For idea-led work, do not begin with sentence polish.
- Write the claim, mechanism, evidence, objection, and implication in plain form first.
- If the idea cannot survive a one-paragraph or one-page explanation, more elegant language is not the fix.
- Compression comes after understanding. Do not compress an idea you have not earned.

## 2. Separate generation from distillation

Conversation, notes, interviews, reading, and observation are generative. Solitary writing and editing are often reductive: competing fragments are compared, weak ideas fall away, and the stronger core becomes visible.

Operational rule:
- Gather broadly.
- Cluster before outlining.
- Distill only after enough material exists to reveal a pattern.
- Preserve unresolved tension until the evidence settles it.

## 3. Voice is discovered through repeated real output

Steph Smith's account of voice is developmental. Distinctiveness emerges through producing work, receiving reactions, noticing what repeatedly feels natural, and leaning into those tendencies. It is not reliably created by choosing three adjectives before writing.

Operational rule:
- Treat voice profiles as evidence accumulated over time.
- Track accepted phrases, rhythms, structures, recurring interests, and reader reactions.
- Let a profile evolve when repeated approved work contradicts the old profile.
- Do not force every piece to exhibit every recorded trait.

A useful test of voice is alignment: the page should plausibly resemble how the person thinks, notices, explains, and speaks when they are not performing for an audience.

## 4. Examples teach texture better than abstract rules

Sam Parr's copywork practice and the Writing Examples corpus point to the same method: study successful examples closely enough to feel how the sentences move. Extract the mechanism rather than copying the surface.

Operational rule:
- Keep an example library by decision type: openings, explanations, transitions, arguments, descriptions, endings, titles, calls to action, research detail, and page architecture.
- When a rule is abstract, attach at least one positive example and one failure mode.
- Prefer a demonstrated mechanism over a style adjective such as "punchy", "smart", "warm", or "elegant".

Do not reproduce copyrighted passages beyond what is necessary for analysis. Store paraphrased mechanisms and short approved snippets.

## 5. Research should appear as precision, not credentials

The research lesson repeated in the Sam Parr discussion is that expertise is more persuasive when the writing contains an observation or detail that could only come from serious investigation. Telling the reader that the writer is experienced is weaker than showing the consequence of that experience.

Operational rule:
- Ask what detail would be impossible to produce from a surface skim.
- Seek primary material, peripheral witnesses, chronology, physical detail, and contradiction when the genre justifies it.
- Let the research change the thesis.
- Never invent a scene, thought, motive, or sensory detail to make research feel vivid.

Robert Greene owns the deeper case-comparison method. This rule concerns how research appears on the page.

## 6. Recognition before correction

Ocean Vuong's workshop method suggests a safeguard against over-editing: identify what the work is already trying to do before correcting it toward a generic standard.

Operational rule:
Before an editing pass, name:
- what feels alive or distinctive;
- the recurring syntactic or structural tendencies;
- the strongest image, observation, or turn;
- what the piece appears to be reaching toward;
- which irregularities may be intentional.

Only then correct defects. Do not assume normalization is improvement.

## 7. Preserve the writer's useful strangeness

A sentence can be clear and still be unusual. Ocean Vuong's argument against homogenized prose is useful here: the aim is not one invisible newspaper sentence for every genre.

Operational rule:
- Keep an unusual construction when it creates a perception the ordinary version cannot.
- Distinguish novelty from obscurity.
- A strange sentence must still reward the reader with a clearer image, feeling, relationship, or thought.
- Do not make every sentence conspicuous. Plain scaffolding gives marked sentences somewhere to land.

## 8. Hold maxims lightly

Adrian Tchaikovsky's warning is structural: "always" and "never" rules fail across sufficiently varied writing. Show versus tell, fixed story structures, planning methods, sentence length, and other craft advice all have counterexamples.

Operational rule:
Every writing maxim has:
- a default;
- a reason the default usually works;
- an exception condition;
- a genre boundary.

If a rule lacks those four parts, treat it as incomplete.

## 9. Copy must start with reader, proposition, then phrasing

Harry Dry's useful sequence is:
1. Who are you talking to?
2. Do you actually have something to say?
3. Then say it well.

His three sentence tests are also useful for copy and public-facing claims:
- Can the reader visualize it?
- Can it be falsified or checked?
- Could a competitor say the same thing unchanged?

Operational rule:
Never use rhetorical craft to compensate for an empty proposition. In commercial writing, proof and ethical persuasion outrank cleverness.

## 10. AI should increase thinking leverage, not replace thought

Nicholas Cole's strongest distinction is between commodity language and attributable thinking. AI is well suited to retrieving, recombining, formatting, adapting, and distributing approved material. It is poorly suited to inventing a person's beliefs, experience, or original point of view on their behalf.

Operational rule:
- Reuse approved language when it already works.
- Ask the human or remain neutral when a new judgment is required.
- Use AI to surface tensions, counterexamples, missing evidence, alternative structures, and questions that help the human think further.
- Treat "sounds polished" as irrelevant if the draft contains no attributable thinking.

## Process sequence

For substantive idea-led writing:
1. Retrieve existing material.
2. Identify the governing idea or Shiny Dime.
3. Build the source packet.
4. Pressure-test the idea with research, counterexamples, and implications.
5. Choose structure from the idea, reader, and genre.
6. Draft in plain language before stylistic escalation.
7. Run the form-match pass.
8. Compare against approved voice evidence.
9. Run recognition-before-correction.
10. Run destructive editing and QA.
11. Red-team consequential work independently.
12. Capture accepted edits as new evidence for the evolving voice profile.

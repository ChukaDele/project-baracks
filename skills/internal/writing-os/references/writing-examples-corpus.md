# Writing Examples corpus: cross-genre craft evidence

Source corpus: the 42 indexed article pages at writingexamples.com/article/, reviewed September 2026.

This is a secondary craft-analysis corpus. Use it to test and expand writing decisions, not as a substitute for primary sources or the intended writer's approved voice.

## Governing finding

Good defaults are not universal laws.

The recurring pattern across the corpus is **form should match meaning, reader, and purpose**. A marked device earns its place when removing it weakens the experience, reasoning, memory, persuasion, or precision of the piece.

The correct question is rarely "is this device allowed?" Ask "what work is this device doing here?"

## Recurrent patterns across genres

### 1. Specific detail carries more meaning than generic praise

Seen in the analyses of Ogilvy/Rolls-Royce, Fitzgerald, Harry Dry/New Balance, the Coca-Cola design brief, Robert Kurson, personal bios, and cover letters.

Use one vivid, falsifiable, or distinctive detail to imply a broader quality. Let the reader infer when inference is stronger than praise.

Tests:
- Can the reader picture the claim?
- Could the claim be tested or contradicted?
- Is the detail specific enough that a competitor or generic writer could not swap in their own name unchanged?
- Is an adjective doing work that a concrete fact could do better?
### 2. Contrast is a thinking tool, not decoration

Seen in Dickens, Tony Bennett, Melville, Gibran, Katy Perry, the infinite-scale example, and Fitzgerald.

Contrast can reveal contradiction, hierarchy, emotional tension, change, or paradox. It works when the relationship between the two sides is itself the idea.

Tests:
- What becomes visible only when these two things are placed beside each other?
- Is the contrast explaining a real tension or merely producing rhetorical symmetry?
- Does the reader understand the consequence of the contrast?

### 3. Syntax and rhythm can embody the subject

Seen in Proust on memory, Gregory David Roberts on infatuation, Poe on madness, Sacks on hallucination, Kerouac on passion, Katy Perry on instability, Melville on the sea, and Borges on dreams.

Long sentences, fragments, repetition, unusual punctuation, circular structure, and deliberate instability can be correct when they reproduce the experience being described.

Tests:
- Does the sentence shape imitate the subject in a way the reader can feel?
- If the prose were normalized, would meaning or experience be lost?
- Is the marked form intentional and local, or merely uncontrolled writing?

### 4. Repetition works when each recurrence compounds meaning

Seen in MLK, Churchill, Huxley, Katy Perry, Melville, and several speech examples.

Useful repetition can create escalation, rhythm, memory, call-and-response, contrast, or inevitability.

Tests:
- Does each repetition raise the stakes, change the context, or intensify the idea?
- Would deleting the repeated phrase reduce momentum or merely remove redundancy?
- Is the repeated phrase simple enough to carry the rhythm?
### 5. Openings need forward pressure

Seen in Seinfeld, Orwell, job descriptions, personal bios, secrets, titles, and copy.

An opening can create curiosity, tension, surprise, specificity, immediate stakes, or a frame-breaking observation. It does not need manufactured drama.

Tests:
- Why should the reader read sentence two?
- Does the opening establish the kind of experience or argument that follows?
- Is surprise relevant to the piece or inserted as bait?
- Could the first paragraph be removed without losing orientation or momentum?

### 6. Compression improves memory when the underlying idea is already clear

Seen in fables, quotable lines, titles, copy, speeches, and Scott Adams-style business writing.

Compression can create portability. A compressed line is useful when it preserves the causal or conceptual core.

Tests:
- Can the idea be repeated accurately after one reading?
- Did compression remove proof, qualification, or necessary context?
- Is the memorable line earned by the argument around it?

### 7. Concrete imagery is a bridge to abstraction

Seen in Gibran, Kurson, Orwell, the infinite-scale example, Fitzgerald, and descriptive pieces.

Personification, analogy, symbol, scene, and physical detail can make an abstract idea apprehensible.

Tests:
- What does the image let the reader understand that an abstract label would not?
- Is the analogy structurally similar to the idea, or merely vivid?
- Does the symbol add a second layer of meaning without requiring explanation to function?
### 8. Distinctiveness beats generic competence

Seen in Harry Dry, personal bios, cover letters, Ogilvy, James Clear, Seinfeld, and the personal-brand material.

A line gains value when it could credibly come from this person, product, situation, or argument and few others.

Tests:
- Could ten competitors say this unchanged?
- Could this sentence have been generated without access to the source packet?
- Does the passage contain a chosen position, lived detail, specific observation, unusual mechanism, or recognisable language?

### 9. Persuasion begins with the reader's state

Seen in Braveheart, Good Will Hunting, MLK, Churchill, job descriptions, cover letters, and titles.

Different readers require different moves. Respect can lower defensiveness. Questions can make the reader complete the argument. Group language can shift identity. Stakes can focus attention. Objections can be addressed before the ask.

Tests:
- What does the reader fear, want, doubt, or already believe?
- Is the writer changing the reader's model or merely asserting harder?
- Does the technique preserve the reader's agency?
- Is the desired action clear?

### 10. A piece needs a governing idea

Seen in fables, titles, speeches, copy, Perell's Shiny Dime, and many descriptive examples.

A strong piece can contain complexity while still orbiting one controlling idea or emotional movement.

Tests:
- What is the smallest viable idea this piece is about?
- Does each section support, complicate, illustrate, test, or extend it?
- Which interesting passage is still a tangent?
## Anti-rules

Do not turn the following defaults into blanket bans.

### "Always use short sentences"
False. Long sentences work when relationships, accumulation, memory, awe, passion, or disorientation require continuity.

Default: keep sentences as simple as the thought allows.

Exception test: does the long sentence let the reader experience a relationship that would be weakened by splitting it?

### "Never repeat yourself"
False. Repetition can create rhythm, escalation, contrast, memory, unity, or emotional force.

Default: remove accidental repetition.

Exception test: does each recurrence add pressure or meaning?

### "Never use rhetorical questions"
False. They can lower defensiveness, invite self-persuasion, create call-and-response, or frame an investigation.

Default: avoid questions that merely simulate engagement.

Exception test: does the question make the reader perform useful cognitive work?

### "Avoid rhetorical symmetry"
False. Chiasmus, parallelism, and antithesis can make a real relationship memorable.

Default: remove decorative symmetry.

Exception test: would the insight survive equally well without the mirrored form? If yes, prefer the plainer version.
### "Avoid metaphor"
False. Metaphor is useful when it converts an abstraction into something the reader can see, feel, or reason about.

Default: reject ornamental metaphor.

Exception test: does the metaphor clarify structure, emotion, scale, or consequence?

### "Always explain explicitly"
False. A precise detail can let the reader infer a stronger conclusion than a paragraph of praise.

Default: be explicit about logic and evidence.

Exception test: is the intended inference clear, fair, and supported?

### "Always write linearly"
False. Dreams, prophecy, memory, interiority, and some narrative forms can benefit from loops, pauses, or non-linear movement.

Default: use the simplest structure that serves comprehension.

Exception test: does the non-linearity reproduce the subject or reveal something a linear sequence would hide?

### "Plain language means plain texture"
False. Ordinary words can still create rhythm, humor, surprise, image, tension, and voice.

Default: use ordinary words when they are exact.

Exception test: is a rarer or stranger word more precise, more visual, or carrying necessary tone?
## Cross-genre QA

Before delivery, ask:

1. What is the governing idea?
2. What is the most concrete piece of evidence or detail?
3. Which sentence could only belong to this piece?
4. Which sentence could be pasted into ten thousand similar pieces?
5. What does the opening make the reader expect?
6. Where does the reader feel forward pressure?
7. Does the structure match the logic or emotional movement?
8. Which stylistic device is most marked?
9. What work is that device doing?
10. If the marked device is removed, what is lost?
11. Is there contrast that reveals a real tension?
12. Is there repetition that compounds rather than repeats?
13. Is an abstraction supported by a scene, mechanism, example, image, or consequence?
14. Can the reader visualize the important claims?
15. For persuasive copy, can important claims be falsified?
16. Could a competitor plausibly say the same line unchanged?
17. Does the writer respect what the reader already knows?
18. Are questions doing reasoning work rather than faking dialogue?
19. Has compression removed needed proof or nuance?
20. Does sentence rhythm follow the thought?
21. Does a long sentence need to be long?
22. Does a short sentence deserve its emphasis?
23. Is a metaphor clarifying or decorating?
24. Is the emotional register earned by the material?
25. Is any personal detail invented or generalized beyond the source?
26. Is the ending the right place for the strongest word, image, or consequence?
27. Does the piece contain a passage that is individually good but irrelevant to the governing idea?
28. Could the reader repeat the central idea accurately?
29. Did the prose become more generic during cleanup?
30. Did the final edit preserve useful idiosyncrasy?
## Genre boundaries

Business/report/proposal:
Favor answer clarity, concrete evidence, direct syntax, selective compression, and reader decision needs. Marked rhetoric must earn its cost.

Personal brand/essay:
Require a real point of view, source-backed personal detail, a Shiny Dime, and enough specificity that the piece could not be written from generic topic knowledge alone.

Copy:
Use the visualize / falsify / distinctiveness test. Pair claims with proof. Do not import hype from unrelated genres.

Speech:
Rhythm, repetition, collective language, and climax matter more than on-page concision. Read aloud.

Narrative/creative nonfiction:
Permit longer sentences, metaphor, ambiguity, non-linearity, and formal play when form embodies experience.

Technical:
Meaning, sequence, safety, and unambiguous action outrank literary texture. Do not transfer creative exceptions into controlled procedures.

# Authority map

Each expert owns a decision type. No expert owns the whole brand.

Format: **Expert — framework — owns — stops at.**

## 1. Insight and brief

**Mark Pollard — The Four Points.** Four sentences: problem, insight, advantage, strategy. From *Strategy Is Your Words* (2020); taught through Sweathead.
Owns: the creative brief, the insight, the problem statement.
Stops at: visual execution, and any claim that an insight is proven. An insight is a bet until tested.

**Michael Johnson — Five and a Half Steps.** Investigate, define strategy and narrative, design, implement, engage. The half step allows parallel work.
Owns: project sequence and the questions asked before design.
Stops at: measurement.

## 2. Positioning and category

**April Dunford — Five (plus one) components.** Competitive alternatives, unique attributes, value and proof, target market characteristics, market category, plus optional trend. From *Obviously Awesome*, with a ten-step process.
Owns: B2B and service positioning. Start here for any paid offer.
Stops at: emotion, culture, and craft. Dunford gives clarity, not distinctiveness.

**Marty Neumeier — brand gap, five disciplines, onlyness.** Brand is a person's gut feeling about a product, service, or organisation. Five disciplines: differentiate, collaborate, innovate, validate, cultivate. Onlyness statement: our [offering] is the only [category] that [benefit] for [audience] in [region] during [time] because [proof].
Owns: the differentiation test after Dunford's clarity test.
Stops at: operational go-to-market detail.

**Fabian Geyrhalter — brand platform sprint.** Compresses purpose, personality, differentiation, audience, and positioning into a short collaborative sprint.
Owns: speed for early-stage founders.
Stops at: rigour. A one-day sprint is a hypothesis.

**Christopher Lochhead — category design (*Play Bigger*).** Use as the opposite view. Dunford usually says pick a known category so buyers understand you. Lochhead says create and own a new one.
Rule: pick a known category when the buyer has a budget line for it. Create a category only when you can fund years of education.

## 3. Architecture

**David Aaker — brand relationship spectrum and brand portfolio.** Treats brands as a system: master brand, sub-brands, endorsed brands, and driver roles.
Owns: portfolio structure and endorsement decisions.
Stops at: craft and modern channel behaviour.

**Rob Meyerson — naming architecture.** Links the naming convention to the architecture, so new offers do not need a new brand each time.
Owns: naming conventions for sub-offers.
Stops at: legal clearance.

## 4. Naming

**Rob Meyerson — *Brand Naming*.** Process discipline: brief, generate, shortlist, prescreen, present, legal clearance, select, launch.
Owns: the process and the decision record.
Stops at: trademark law. A lawyer clears a name, not a namer.

**Alexandra Watkins — SMILE and SCRATCH.** SMILE: Suggestive, Meaningful, Imagery, Legs, Emotional. SCRATCH: Spelling-challenged, Copycat, Restrictive, Annoying, Tame, Curse of knowledge, Hard to pronounce.
Owns: the screening test for candidate names.
Stops at: B2B and technical categories, where a "tame" name can still be correct. Also stops at legal risk.
Note: SCRATCH's R is **Restrictive**, not "Random".

**Anthony Shore, Nancy Friedman, Christopher Johnson.** Professional namers and language analysts. Use their published work for linguistic checks: sound symbolism, morpheme mix, spoken clarity, and cross-language risk.
Stops at: strategy.

## 5. Verbal identity

**Mark Pollard.** Point of view and strategy language. See section 1.

**Donald Miller — StoryBrand SB7.** Seven-part frame: character, problem, guide, plan, call to action, avoided failure, success. Customer is the hero.
Owns: conversion clarity on landing pages and sales pages.
Stops at: distinctiveness. SB7 makes many sites sound identical. Use it for structure, not for voice.

**Bernadette Jiwa — story-driven strategy.** Empathy and narrative.
Owns: humanising dry categories.
Stops at: proof. A story does not replace evidence.

**John Simmons and Nick Parker — verbal identity craft.** Tone-of-voice principles, word choice, and rhythm. Simmons's work with 26 and *We, Me, Them & It*.
Owns: voice rules that a writer can actually follow.
Stops at: positioning.

## 6. Visual direction

**Chris Do and Jose Caballer — Stylescapes, taught at The Futur by Ben Burns.** Chris Do and Jose Caballer coined the term. A Stylescape is a wide, composed board that shows a full visual world: image direction, colour, type, texture, and application. Two or three boards give the client a compass direction before any logo work.
Owns: visual direction and client alignment before craft.
Stops at: empirical proof of distinctiveness, and typographic detail.

**Michael Johnson.** Keeps visual work tied to the written strategy. See section 1.

## 7. Typography

**Ellen Lupton — *Thinking with Type*.** Type families, scale, kerning, tracking, line length, grid, alignment, and hierarchy. Her pairing rule: contrast enough to be clear, share enough to look intentional.
Owns: typographic logic and hierarchy.
Stops at: business strategy.

**Paula Scher — type as spirit.** Type can be the whole identity, not a label on it.
Owns: scale, boldness, and typographic identity systems.
Stops at: small-business budget reality and accessibility.

**Erik Spiekermann — type in systems.** Performance of type in real use: signage, screens, small sizes, licensing, and web loading.
Owns: type that survives production.
Stops at: brand meaning.

## 8. Colour

**Josef Albers — *Interaction of Color*.** Colour is relative. The same colour changes with its neighbour, its area, and its light. Test colour in context and in pairs.
Owns: how colour actually behaves.
Stops at: brand ownership questions and screen accessibility standards.

**WCAG 2.2 contrast rules.** Not a person, but the binding constraint. Check text contrast and non-text contrast before you approve a palette.
Owns: accessibility pass or fail.
Stops at: taste.

**Jenni Romaniuk.** Asks whether colour can become an owned asset. See section 10.

Rule: no colour-emotion chart is authoritative. "Blue means trust" is not evidence. Category context beats generic colour psychology.

## 9. Logo and symbol

**Sagi Haviv (Chermayeff & Geismar & Haviv) — three criteria.** Appropriate, distinctive and memorable, simple in form. A logo identifies. It does not explain the business.
Owns: mark quality judgement.
Stops at: strategy and system design.

**Martina Flor — lettering and logotype craft.** Use when the mark is drawn type.
Stops at: strategy.

**James Martin (Made by James) and Aaron Draplin.** Sketch volume and vector discipline. Use as production practice, not as decision authority.

## 10. Distinctive assets and evidence

**Jenni Romaniuk — Distinctive Asset Grid.** Two measures: Fame (how many buyers link the asset to your brand) and Uniqueness (how exclusively they name only your brand). High Fame plus high Uniqueness is the target. High Uniqueness with low Fame is worth investment. High Fame with low Uniqueness is a category cue, not your asset. Build a palette of assets, not one logo.
Owns: whether an identity element is actually working.
Stops at: creative generation. The grid judges; it does not invent.

**Byron Sharp and Ehrenberg-Bass — *How Brands Grow*.** Mental availability, physical availability, category entry points, and consistent distinctive assets.
Owns: the empirical counterweight to taste.
Stops at: very small brands and new categories, where the laws are based on large-brand data. Say this out loud when the client is a startup.

**Adam Morgan — *Eating the Big Fish*.** Challenger strategy for brands with less money than the leader.
Owns: behaviour when you cannot outspend anyone.
Stops at: measurement.

**Rory Sutherland — *Alchemy*.** Signalling, costly signals, and psycho-logic. Use as the counterweight to pure rationality.
Stops at: anything presented as proof.

## 11. Identity system

**Alina Wheeler — *Designing Brand Identity*.** Five phases: research, clarify strategy, design identity, create touchpoints, manage assets. Also the best checklist source for deliverables.
Owns: the deliverable list and the brand guide structure.
Stops at: original creative thinking.

**Chris Do / The Futur.** Application sets: logo, lock-ups, patterns, gradients, type, cards, environmental, social, site, documents.
Owns: application coverage.
Stops at: governance.

**Wally Olins.** Behaviour, culture, and organisational reality of identity.
Owns: whether the company can live the brand.
Stops at: craft.

## 12. Personal brand

**Dorie Clark — *Stand Out*.** Breakthrough idea, then content, then media and speaking, then network and platform.
Owns: authority building for a person.
Stops at: company identity.

**Daniel Priestley — Key Person of Influence: Pitch, Publish, Product, Profile, Partnership.**
Owns: the packaging of a personal brand into an offer ecosystem.
Stops at: proof of demand. Publishing is not selling.

Rule: keep the founder brand separate from the company brand. Say which one owns each asset.

## 13. Deliberate omissions and demotions

**Kaye Putnam and Bethany McCamish — Jungian archetypes (12 types).** Keep as a secondary creative lens only, used after positioning. Archetypes help a team decide how to behave and sound. They are not customer evidence and must never overrule data on buyers, competitors, or category.

**Colour psychology as a foundation.** Rejected. Replaced by Albers (behaviour), WCAG (accessibility), and Romaniuk (ownership).

**Type-only or logo-only coaching as strategy.** Rejected. Craft experts stay inside craft.

## Correction log

- The Stylescape term comes from Chris Do and Jose Caballer at Blind, and is taught at The Futur by Ben Burns. Melinda Livsey teaches brand strategy through Marks and Maker; she did not coin Stylescapes.
- Neumeier's terms are "brand gap", "five disciplines", "onlyness", "trueline". "Brand Bridge" is not his framework.
- SCRATCH = Spelling-challenged, Copycat, **Restrictive**, Annoying, Tame, Curse of knowledge, Hard to pronounce.
- Romaniuk's grid axes are **Fame** and **Uniqueness**, not "uniqueness and prevalence".

# Presentation Storylining

## Goal

Build presentations that think before they decorate.

The output should feel like a strong strategist and editor built the argument first, then a presentation designer rendered it. A beautiful deck with weak logic is a failure. A rigorous deck with unreadable communication is also a failure.

Default principle:

`decision -> structure -> evidence -> synthesis -> storyline -> slide proof -> critic -> render`

Do not begin with layouts, templates, colours or slide generation unless the user explicitly asks for a purely visual treatment of already-final content.

## 0. Boundary and composition

This skill owns **what the deck should argue, in what order, and what each slide must prove**.

It does not replace:
- `knowledge-work` for substantial research, source collection, market analysis or decision reasoning;
- `source-ingestion` for retrieving named source material;
- `design-direction-and-taste` for art direction and visual grammar;
- platform-specific slide/PPTX/Figma/Canva tooling for rendering;
- `pdf-reporting-qa` for PDF-specific output checks.

For evidence-heavy decks, load `knowledge-work` first or in parallel. For substantive visual creation, load `design-direction-and-taste` after the ghost deck/storyline passes.

Do not create a separate strategy framework merely because the output is slides. Use existing reasoning capabilities and transform their conclusions into presentation logic here.

## 1. Classify the deck by job

Infer the deck's primary job before structuring it:

- **DECIDE** — board, strategy, investment, transformation, pricing, operating decision. Audience must choose or approve something.
- **PERSUADE** — sales, proposal, fundraising, partnership. Audience must believe and act.
- **ALIGN** — executive update, project steering, operating review. Audience must share a view of status, risks and next actions.
- **TEACH** — onboarding, training, explainer. Audience must understand and retain a model or process.
- **SHOW** — portfolio, case study, conference/demo. Audience must understand or remember the work.

A deck can mix jobs, but one must dominate. The dominant job determines what belongs in the main flow versus appendix.

For DECIDE/PERSUADE decks, default to consulting-grade storylining unless the brief clearly calls for another narrative form.

## 2. Frame the decision before the story

Write a concise internal Deck Brief before slide planning:

- audience and their current state;
- decision/action required;
- governing question;
- what is at stake;
- constraints and non-negotiables;
- evidence available vs missing;
- desired outcome after the final slide.

If the audience is senior/executive, optimise for decision-useful compression, not exhaustive explanation.

Do not ask the user for information that can be inferred from the brief or source material. Surface a gap only if it materially changes the argument.

## 3. Structure the problem, not the slides

For decision-oriented decks, decompose the governing question into a small set of load-bearing issues. Use MECE as a diagnostic, not a religion.

Good structure:
- collectively covers the decision-critical space;
- minimises overlap;
- exposes assumptions;
- separates causes, effects and remedies;
- can be tested with evidence.

Avoid framework salad. Do not insert 2x2s, Five Forces, SWOT, value chains or other frameworks unless they reduce uncertainty or improve the decision.

If no named framework fits cleanly, use first-principles decomposition.

## 4. Form and attack a Day-1 hypothesis when appropriate

For DECIDE/PERSUADE decks, form a provisional answer early when enough context exists.

Express:
- the current best answer;
- 2-4 load-bearing sub-hypotheses;
- confidence in each;
- what evidence would disprove each;
- what remains genuinely unknown.

Then prioritise analysis by expected information value: attack assumptions that are both uncertain and decision-critical first.

Do not become attached to the initial hypothesis. Evidence that kills the story is useful evidence.

For TEACH/SHOW decks, a Day-1 hypothesis may be unnecessary; use a learning or narrative objective instead.

## 5. Enforce evidence discipline

Every material claim should have an evidence status. Use these labels internally when useful:

- **FACT** — directly supported by credible evidence/source.
- **INFERENCE** — reasoned conclusion from facts.
- **ASSUMPTION** — necessary but unverified premise.
- **ESTIMATE** — quantified approximation with method/range.

Never let an assumption become a fact merely because it is written in an action title.

For consequential decks:
- preserve provenance for load-bearing claims;
- expose material source gaps;
- distinguish observed data from interpretation;
- show ranges/sensitivity where false precision would mislead.

## 6. Synthesize; do not summarize

Before writing slide titles, reduce the analysis into the few insights that change what the audience should believe or do.

A synthesis insight should answer at least one:
- What changed?
- Why does it matter?
- What explains it?
- What should we do differently because of it?
- What decision does it unlock?

If a point merely repeats source material without changing the decision, it is probably summary and belongs in notes, backup or nowhere.

Prefer 3-5 load-bearing insights to 15 equally weighted observations.

## 7. Write the governing thought

Write one sentence that captures the deck's central answer or intended audience conclusion.

Good governing thoughts are:
- specific;
- consequential;
- supportable;
- directional;
- compact enough to guide slide selection.

Examples of form, not templates:
- `We should enter segment X now because A and B create an advantaged window, while risk C can be bounded.`
- `The programme is off-track because constraint A, not execution volume, is the bottleneck; reallocating resources to B restores the target.`

If the governing thought cannot yet be written, the analysis is not ready for a final deck.

## 8. Choose a narrative spine

Use the minimum structure needed to make the argument inevitable.

Common spines:

### Answer-first pyramid
Use for executives and recommendations:
1. answer / recommendation;
2. 2-4 supporting arguments;
3. evidence beneath each;
4. implications / action.

### Situation -> complication -> resolution
Use when change/tension must be established before the answer:
1. current reality;
2. what changed / why status quo fails;
3. governing question;
4. answer and proof.

### Evidence -> insight -> implication
Use for research-heavy or diagnostic decks:
1. signal/evidence;
2. what it means;
3. consequence;
4. action.

### Problem -> mechanism -> intervention -> outcome
Use for product/operating/transformation decks.

Do not mechanically use SCQA, Pyramid Principle or MECE if they make the story less natural. Frameworks serve the decision.

## 9. Build a ghost deck before slide content

Create the deck as **titles only** first.

Each ghost-deck line should be a proposed slide title that states the conclusion of that slide. Read the titles in sequence with all body content hidden.

The title sequence must:
- tell a coherent story by itself;
- answer the governing question;
- progress rather than repeat;
- contain no unexplained logical jumps;
- end with a clear implication, decision or next action where relevant.

Do not proceed to full slide content until the ghost deck passes horizontal logic.

### Horizontal-logic test

Ask:
- If the audience read only the titles, would they understand the argument?
- Does each title earn the next one?
- Is any slide merely a topic label such as `Market Overview`, `Our Approach`, `Financials`, or `Next Steps` when a conclusion is available?
- Can any slide be removed without weakening the story?
- Is a missing bridge slide forcing the audience to infer too much?

Prefer assertion-led titles:

Weak: `Customer retention`

Better: `Retention falls sharply after month three, driven primarily by failed activation rather than pricing`

## 10. Design slide proof before visual layout

For each slide, create a compact Slide Blueprint:

- **Action title:** the conclusion.
- **Purpose:** why this slide exists in the story.
- **Proof:** 1-3 evidence elements needed to support the title.
- **Evidence type:** data / quote / comparison / process / case / calculation / source.
- **Implication:** what the audience should infer or do.
- **Confidence/gap:** any material caveat.
- **Likely archetype:** chart, table, comparison, process, timeline, exhibit, narrative, case study, etc.

The slide body exists to prove the title. Do not write the body first and invent a headline afterwards.

### Vertical-logic test

For every slide:
- Does the evidence actually prove the action title?
- Is the title stronger than the evidence warrants?
- Is the most important proof visually/structurally primary?
- Are annotations explaining meaning rather than restating numbers?
- Would deleting a bullet weaken the proof? If not, delete it.

## 11. Executive compression rules

For senior audiences:
- lead with the answer;
- make trade-offs explicit;
- quantify magnitude where decision-relevant;
- distinguish reversible vs irreversible choices;
- surface the 1-3 real risks, not a ceremonial risk list;
- say what would change the recommendation;
- move methodology and exhaustive evidence to appendix unless required for trust.

A board/CEO deck should not require the audience to perform the synthesis themselves.

## 12. Handle common deck types deliberately

### Board / strategy
Main flow usually needs:
- decision/context;
- governing thought;
- evidence-backed diagnosis/opportunity;
- alternatives/trade-offs;
- recommendation;
- economics/impact;
- risks and mitigations;
- decision required / next actions.

### Investor / fundraising
Main flow usually needs:
- important problem/change;
- unique insight / why now;
- solution/mechanism;
- evidence of demand/traction;
- market/economics;
- moat/right-to-win;
- team/ability to execute;
- ask/use of funds/milestones.

Do not force fundraising conventions when evidence does not support them.

### Sales / proposal
Main flow usually needs:
- client's world/problem;
- cost/risk of current state;
- desired outcome;
- proposed mechanism;
- proof / credibility;
- implementation path;
- economics/terms where relevant;
- clear next action.

### Executive update
Main flow usually needs:
- BLUF/status;
- what changed since last review;
- outcome vs plan;
- root causes;
- decisions/blockers;
- next actions, owner and timing.

### Training / onboarding
Optimise for comprehension and recall rather than consultant density. Use progressive disclosure, examples and exercises. Do not impose board-deck compression.

## 13. Run the critic before rendering

For consequential decks, use a reviewer that did not author the main storyline when practical.

Critique across seven dimensions:

1. **Decision clarity** — Is the audience/action explicit?
2. **Argument validity** — Do conclusions follow from evidence?
3. **Horizontal logic** — Do titles alone tell the story?
4. **Vertical logic** — Does each slide prove its title?
5. **Evidence integrity** — Facts/inferences/assumptions/estimates separated?
6. **Executive usefulness** — Are trade-offs, risks and asks explicit?
7. **Compression** — Can slides/words be removed without loss?

Also run an adversarial question:

`What would a skeptical CEO/CFO/partner/investor challenge first, and what evidence would change the recommendation?`

Do not let the author be the only judge of a high-consequence deck.

## 14. Rendering handoff

Only after storyline and proof pass:

1. hand the Slide Blueprints to the design/rendering layer;
2. preserve action titles and evidence hierarchy;
3. choose chart/diagram/layout based on the proof, not decoration;
4. keep data and text editable where the chosen output format supports it;
5. visually inspect the rendered deck for overflow, hierarchy and evidence readability;
6. if visual edits alter meaning, rerun vertical logic on affected slides.

Presentation aesthetics should serve the argument. Do not imitate McKinsey/BCG/Bain branding or visual style unless the user explicitly asks for a stylistic reference; the transferable value is the thinking discipline.

## 15. Appendix discipline

Use appendix to preserve credibility without burdening the main narrative.

Move items to appendix when they are:
- methodological detail;
- secondary analyses;
- sensitivity tables;
- source notes;
- alternative scenarios;
- evidence needed only if challenged.

Do not hide material contradictions, decision-critical caveats or risks in appendix.

## 16. Failure conditions

This skill has failed when:
- slide creation begins before the decision/story is understood;
- titles are mostly topics rather than assertions;
- the title sequence does not form a complete argument;
- slides contain evidence that does not support their headline;
- research is dumped into slides instead of synthesized;
- a framework is used because it is prestigious rather than useful;
- assumptions are presented as facts;
- the deck reaches a recommendation without considering credible alternatives or disconfirming evidence;
- the main narrative is exhaustive instead of decision-useful;
- design polish masks weak logic;
- the audience must perform the final synthesis themselves;
- the deck copies consultancy aesthetics while missing consultancy reasoning.

## 17. Stop conditions

Stop expanding the deck when:
- the governing question is answered;
- each material claim has enough evidence for the stakes;
- titles alone tell the story;
- each slide proves its title;
- credible objections are addressed or explicitly bounded;
- the audience can identify the required decision/action;
- additional slides would add completeness but not change understanding or action.

Prefer a shorter defensible deck over a longer impressive-looking one.

## Resolver examples

### Should trigger

- "Turn this analysis into a board deck and make the argument airtight."
- "Build a McKinsey-style strategy presentation from these findings — I care about the thinking, not their branding."
- "Create a pitch deck where the slide titles alone tell the investment story."
- "Convert this proposal into an executive deck with clear recommendations and evidence."
- "Review my deck for storyline, action titles and whether each slide proves its point."

### Should not trigger

- "Make a 5-slide photo montage from my holiday pictures."
- "Change the font and background colour on this already-final deck."
- "Export this existing presentation to PDF without changing content."
- "Generate one decorative Instagram carousel from this quote."

### Conflicts and composition

- `knowledge-work`: owns research/decision analysis; this skill consumes its conclusions and evidence. Use both for evidence-heavy strategic decks.
- `design-direction-and-taste`: owns visual art direction; load after storylining, not before, unless evaluating an already-rendered deck's visual system.
- `pdf-reporting-qa`: owns PDF-specific QA after export.
- generic presentation/rendering tooling: owns file generation and editability, not the argument.

When conflict exists, **truth and decision logic outrank visual convention**.

## Provenance and adaptation

This skill synthesizes recurring consulting communication practices and ideas observed in open-source consulting skill/toolkit projects, including issue/hypothesis trees, synthesis, Pyramid/SCQA-style storylining, ghost decks, horizontal/vertical logic and adversarial critique. It intentionally adapts concepts rather than cloning consultancy branding or copying a third-party deck template.

Maintain the skill by learning from actual deck outcomes:
- user corrections to storyline or recommendation;
- slides repeatedly removed/rewritten;
- objections raised by real audiences;
- evidence gaps that caused loss of trust;
- deck archetypes that consistently improve decisions.

Promote patterns only after recurrence/validation under Major's `learning-capture` and `skillify` rules.
